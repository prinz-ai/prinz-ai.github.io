A crown, a conversion, a deadline

France had a hereditary king whom much of the Catholic League refused to recognise. Henry of Navarre claimed the crown as Henry IV; the League wanted a Catholic ruler. In July 1593, his approaching conversion threatened to take away the strongest reason for rejecting him.[4][5]

Desportes writes from Mayenne’s circle at this turning point. His two recipients offer different routes into Rome: Frachetta, an agent and confidant, and Pietro Aldobrandini, the Pope’s nephew. A contemporary abstract dates both letters to 22 July, three days before the ceremony at Saint-Denis.[1][2][3]

The letters’ central argument is practical: without money, troops and clear papal guarantees, the League cannot keep rejecting peace. Desportes depicts the legate as serving Spain at Mayenne’s expense. Read this as a plea for support and control over negotiations, with accusations that require separate corroboration. Aldobrandini, 25–43 ↗ · Frachetta, 28–33 ↗

  1. 1588–1589

    Henri de Guise is killed; Mayenne takes over the League. Henry III’s death then makes the Protestant Henry of Navarre the hereditary claimant to France.[8][4]

  2. January 1593

    The League’s Estates General meet in Paris to find a Catholic sovereign. Hereditary right, religion and Spanish influence collide.[5]

  3. 28 June

    The Parlement of Paris’s arrêt Lemaistre insists on Catholic and French kingship and rejects agreements handing the crown to a foreign prince or princess.[7]

  4. 22 July

    Desportes sends these two letters from Paris. His longer letter says Navarre will attend Mass on Sunday. Read the prediction ↗[1]

  5. 25 July

    Henry abjures Protestantism at Saint-Denis. Conversion begins to remove the League’s central religious objection; it does not instantly end the war.[5]

  6. 31 July

    Henry and Mayenne agree to a three-month truce. Desportes had anticipated one in the Frachetta letter.[6] Read his expectation ↗

  7. 1594–1598

    Henry is crowned at Chartres and enters Paris in 1594; Rome absolves him in 1595. The Edict of Nantes and peace with Spain at Vervins follow in 1598.[5][9][4]

The people and their aims

DesportesSender · Mayenne’s secretary

Writing from Paris, Desportes defends Mayenne and seeks a reliable channel to the Pope. Enzo Baldini’s biography of Frachetta identifies the secretary as Thibault Desportes and records his Roman missions in 1590–91 and 1592–93. That identification is attributed here to Baldini; the manuscript attribution gives the surname Desportes.[1][2]

His immediate purpose is visible in the longer letter: persuade Pietro Aldobrandini that the League cannot fight on with promises alone, defend his master’s reputation, and ask for papal instructions through himself, bypassing Sega. This is an advocate’s account, not a neutral report. Aldobrandini, verso 25–33 ↗

Girolamo FrachettaRecipient · political writer and agent in Rome

Frachetta (1558–1619) wrote about government and war and worked in Roman patronage and information networks. He became Mayenne’s informer and managed French postal business in Rome, a position giving him access to news and political contacts.[2]

Desportes addresses him with greater familiarity than Aldobrandini, mixing political grievance with news of Frachetta’s unpaid pension. In September 1593, Frachetta supplied his correspondence with Mayenne and Desportes to the Spanish ambassador, the Duke of Sessa, through Cardinal Sauli. Baldini links this to his opposition to Mayenne’s obstruction of the Spanish marriage scheme. The source does not establish that this particular surviving sheet was among those handed over.[2] Frachetta, 23–32 ↗

Pietro AldobrandiniRecipient · the Pope’s nephew in Rome

Pietro (1571–1621), nephew of Clement VIII, handled France, Spain and Savoy in the papal secretariat. His cousin Cinzio handled other territories. Pietro was only twenty-two when this letter arrived; he became a cardinal on 17 September 1593, almost two months later.[3]

This explains the letter’s mixture of deference and pressure: Pietro could bring Desportes’s complaints to the Pope. Desportes also reports that Henry’s supporters claim to possess reassuring letters from Pietro and Clement. These are reported claims within the letter, not independently authenticated promises. Aldobrandini, 28–29 and 44–48 ↗

Charles de Lorraine, Duke of MayenneLeague leader · Desportes’s master

Mayenne (1554–1611) took charge of the Catholic League after his brother Henri de Guise was killed in 1588. His position depended on keeping together a coalition opposed to a Protestant king while retaining influence over its choice of a Catholic successor.[8]

Desportes presents him as France’s exhausted protector, willing even to accept his nephew Charles de Guise as king if real military protection is supplied. This is also a defence against accusations that Mayenne’s own ambitions were obstructing the election. The letter’s praise tells us what Desportes wanted Rome to believe. Aldobrandini, verso 20–24 ↗

Henry IV · “Navarre”Bourbon claimant and the League’s enemy

Henry (1553–1610) was king of Navarre and the hereditary successor to Henry III, whose death in 1589 ended the Valois kings’ rule. The League rejected him because he was Protestant, although Catholics also served him. Desportes’s continued use of “Navarre” withholds the French royal title claimed by his opponent.[4]

By July 1593 conversion offered Henry a way to separate opponents of Protestant kingship from opponents of his dynasty. Desportes sees the danger clearly: an enemy who becomes Catholic can win over former enemies. Aldobrandini, 21–24 ↗

Clement VIII · Ippolito AldobrandiniPope · the ultimate audience

Clement (1536–1605), Pope from 1592, wanted Catholic rule in France and the preservation of papal authority. He initially supported the League, but Henry’s growing strength and conversion eventually made reconciliation possible. Papal absolution came on 17 September 1595, not at the July 1593 ceremony.[9]

Desportes tries to separate the Pope’s interests from the conduct of his agents: loyal service to Rome, he argues, requires correcting the legate. The two letters also refer to a fuller report addressed directly to the Pope. Frachetta, 28–33 ↗

Filippo Sega · Cardinal of PiacenzaThe papal legate in France

Sega (1537–1596), bishop of Piacenza and cardinal from 1591, became Clement’s legate in 1592. His earlier diplomacy included service in Spain; his Spanish sympathies are documented independently of these letters. He resisted acceptance of Henry’s conversion on the terms set by French clergy.[10]

Desportes accuses him of favouring Spain, pressing an impracticable royal election and obstructing useful papal briefs. Those charges are Desportes’s case against him. The g-like sign represents the office “le légat”; it does not give a personal initial. Frachetta, 28–32 ↗ · Aldobrandini, verso 29–33 ↗

Philip II of SpainSpanish king · military patron and dynastic rival

Philip (1527–1598) backed the League and promoted his daughter Isabella Clara Eugenia for the French succession. A friendly Catholic monarchy in France would strengthen Spain’s position; a French settlement on Henry’s terms would weaken its leverage. Spanish military aid therefore came with political aims.[26]

Desportes complains that Philip’s army has done little, and that promises cannot keep the League together. The phrase “the Spaniard” refers to the Spanish interest in this political argument; it need not mean every occurrence encodes Philip personally. Aldobrandini, 19–27 ↗

Isabella Clara EugeniaThe Infanta · proposed queen of France

Isabella (1566–1633) was Philip II’s daughter. “Infanta” is a royal title, not her name.[11] Her mother, Elisabeth of Valois, was Henry III’s sister: Spain’s candidate belonged to the French royal family through the female line. That connection supplied a dynastic argument, but did not overcome the Salic rule excluding succession through women.[27]

One scheme married her to a French prince; the young Duke of Guise became the proposed partner. Desportes says he had earlier sought such a marriage but received no firm commitment. The demand for a Catholic and French king made the proposal contentious.[6][7] Aldobrandini, 32–35 ↗

Charles de Lorraine, Duke of GuiseMayenne’s nephew · proposed king

The Guise discussed here is Charles (1571–1640), the fourth duke, son of Henri de Guise, assassinated in 1588. He was Mayenne’s nephew. Keeping father and son separate matters: it is the young Charles whose possible kingship and marriage are being negotiated.[12][13]

Desportes says Mayenne would accept him if his supporters could explain how to defend him. In this account, the missing ingredient is credible force, not another promise. Aldobrandini, verso 20–24 ↗

Lorenzo Suárez de Figueroa, Duke of FeriaPhilip II’s representative in France

The second Duke of Feria (1559–1607) was a Spanish diplomat sent to France during the succession negotiations. His speech to the Estates on 2 April 1593 survives in print alongside Philip II’s letter.[14][15]

Desportes says the Infanta’s marriage question was referred to Feria and the legate: one channel to Spanish power, the other to papal authority. He wanted firmer commitments from Rome itself. Aldobrandini, 33–38 ↗

Girolamo Agucchi · AgocchiThe legate’s nephew

Agucchi was Sega’s nephew and an intermediary in papal affairs. British Library descriptions of his April 1593 letters document negotiations with Mayenne and disputes with Malvasia over money and troops. These closely corroborate the setting of Desportes’s reference to the nephew’s complaints.[16] Aldobrandini, 17–18 ↗

Innocenzo Malvasia · “Malvoisie”Papal military commissioner

Malvasia (1552–1612) took over the papal army commission from Girolamo Matteucci on 21 December 1592. His office and quarrel with Mayenne make him the well-supported identification of “Malvoisie.” Independent correspondence records Mayenne’s anger over the dismissal of papal troops.[17][18]

Desportes admits he was absent when the disagreement occurred. His report mixes a complaint received from Agucchi with general reputation: the letter itself tells us the limits of his direct knowledge. Aldobrandini, 16–18 ↗

Girolamo Matteucci · probably “Mateouci”Earlier papal military commissioner

Matteucci preceded Malvasia in the administration of papal troops in France. That sequence makes him the plausible historical identification of the letter’s “Mateouci / Mateuci.” The biographical fit is stronger than the spelling correspondence, so this identification remains qualified.[17]

Desportes uses the earlier official to frame a history of poor papal administration, even offering to defend his accusations in person. Aldobrandini, 12–16 ↗

Gregory XIV · Niccolò SfondratiThe earlier Pope who sent aid

Gregory XIV (1535–1591), Pope in 1590–91, financed an expedition to support the League and Spain in 1591. This makes him the historical referent of Desportes’s comparison with spending under “Pope Gregory.”[19]

The source writes the name without a regnal number. The identification comes from the chronology. Gregory’s documented military expenditure must not be conflated with the separate 500,000-écu claim in the Frachetta letter. Aldobrandini, 30–32 ↗

[Gondi] · probably Girolamo, or JérômeA contextual identification, still bracketed

Letters from Henry IV in April and May 1593 connect Jérôme Gondi with the conversion negotiations. They support the proposed identification of the journey Desportes condemns, but they do not assign a value to its three-upright cipher sign.[20][21]

The reading therefore stays [Gondi]. It should not silently become Pierre, Cardinal de Gondi, or Albert de Gondi: those are different people. Desportes attributes the conversion and the withdrawal of 500,000 écus to the journey; that causal claim and the exact financial transaction remain unverified. Frachetta, 26–28 ↗

Doctor GratianA theatrical nickname; the person is unidentified

“Doctor Gratian” belongs to the comic Dottore tradition. A figure in the Recueil Fossard bears this name, which fits Desportes’s joke about someone playing his part. It does not establish the identity of the real person he was mocking.[22] Frachetta, 5–7 ↗

Poquinteste and the unnamed circleReadable references without secure biographies

Poquinteste, “our uncle,” the man at the college, the man of La Chesne, “the monarch,” “your auditor,” and the pension’s prelate remain insufficiently identified. The nickname “uncle” need not establish kinship. The unnamed Bourbon candidate and the candidate proposed by the Pope also should not be assigned names solely because a known contender seems to fit. Frachetta, 8–24 ↗ · Aldobrandini, 32–43 ↗

These are gaps in historical identification, not reasons to erase the words that are readable. The letters assume knowledge shared by sender and recipient which a modern reader may no longer possess.

A French war with European stakes

Spain and the northern frontier. Philip had already sent Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, into France from the Spanish Netherlands, even as Spain faced the Dutch revolt. French intervention competed with that northern war for attention and resources. Spain was both a military patron and a rival source of political direction.[26] Aldobrandini, 19–20 ↗

England and the Protestant powers. Elizabeth I supported Henry with aid reaching him through Normandy. A French royal succession therefore mattered across the Channel as well as in Rome and Madrid. Henry’s coalition included Catholics, so the war cannot be mapped simply as every Catholic against every Protestant.[5][4]

Rome and the Ottoman frontier. Clement hoped to unite Christian powers against the Ottoman Empire. He wanted Spain to reduce its commitments in the Netherlands and support that wider struggle, but Spanish priorities did not simply follow papal wishes. This helps explain why Desportes appeals to the interests of “Christendom” while accusing papal agents of following Spain: shared religion did not guarantee shared strategy.[9] Aldobrandini, verso 1–7 ↗

Money as a test of credibility. Desportes contrasts promises with resources. Frachetta’s pension and the reported withdrawal of 500,000 écus show the personal and military sides of that dependence. An écu was a coin and monetary denomination; the letters do not identify the exact payment arrangement behind this amount.[25] Frachetta, 23–28 ↗

What the cipher adds

Tomokiyo’s reconstructed key gives two possible letters for many signs. French words and manuscript comparisons select the reading; period writers such as Montaigne help with expressions. Those literary parallels illuminate language, not the identities of secret correspondents.[23][24]

The manuscript views keep that evidence beside the ink. Brackets disclose supplies, including [Gondi]; historical interpretation does not silently turn them into certain decipherments.
