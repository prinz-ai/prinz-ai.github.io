The Catholic League Letters (1593)

In July 1593, France is exhausted by more than three decades of religious war. For nearly eight years, the Protestant Henry of Navarre, king of France as Henry IV since 1589, has fought the Catholic League, a coalition of Catholic princes, nobles and towns that refuses to accept him as king. Led first by Henri I, Duke of Guise, and, after his assassination in 1588, by his brother Charles, Duke of Mayenne, the League relies on Spanish military aid and papal support.

That alliance is coming apart. Rome’s backing has served both the French Catholic leaders and their Spanish ally; Pope Gregory XIV even sent troops and money to sustain their war. But by 1593, Mayenne’s circle increasingly sees papal policy as favoring Spain. Filippo Sega, the representative of Pope Clement VIII in France, is closely aligned with Spanish interests. Mayenne depends on Spain’s assistance, yet resists a settlement that would place the French crown under Spanish control and diminish his own authority.

The dispute centers on the Estates General assembled in Paris to choose a Catholic king. Philip II of Spain promotes his daughter, the Infanta Isabella Clara Eugenia, whose mother was a French princess. Her claim runs against the Salic rule excluding women and succession through the female line. Marriage to a Catholic prince offers a political way around these objections, though it cannot simply remove them. Spain first proposes Archduke Ernest of Austria as her husband, then the young Charles, Duke of Guise, Mayenne’s nephew.

Mayenne presses competing candidates, including his own son, and bargains over the conditions of any election. His resistance is not an absolute refusal of an Infanta marriage: Desportes says he sought a Bourbon candidate and then a French prince for her husband. He also insists that Mayenne was willing to accept Guise as king, provided his allies supplied the means to defend him. Promises alone would no longer suffice.

Henry’s announced conversion to Catholicism offers another path to peace and recognition of his royal rights, including reconciliation with the Pope. For Mayenne, it threatens both the League’s cause and his own power: once Henry becomes Catholic, the strongest religious argument against accepting him disappears. The conversion will take place at Saint-Denis on 25 July; papal absolution will follow only in 1595.

On 22 July, just three days before the conversion, Mayenne’s secretary, Thibault Desportes, writes these two letters from Paris. One goes to Pietro Aldobrandini, the Pope’s nephew responsible for French affairs; the other to Girolamo Frachetta, Mayenne’s agent in Rome. Desportes defends his master’s conduct, attacks the allies he believes have abandoned him, and explains why a truce may now be unavoidable. The letters reveal the divisions within the Catholic alliance at the moment Henry’s conversion threatens to undo it.

They also put a precise figure on the financial stakes. Desportes associates the journey that brought about Henry’s conversion with the withdrawal of 500,000 écus. Henry’s own letter of 26 April 1593 links his promised conversion to military aid and a request for 200,000 écus in additional cash. Read together, the documents suggest that Desportes’s larger figure may relate to the financial bargain behind the conversion. The amount is explicit; whether it represents a total payment to Henry remains an interpretation, since Desportes does not identify the payer or recipient.

The Frachetta letter offers a possible explanation for a later betrayal. Desportes turns down Frachetta’s request for a pension, saying that the money cannot be obtained from the prelate responsible. In September, Frachetta hands his correspondence with Desportes and Mayenne to the Spanish ambassador. His opposition to Mayenne’s policy is documented; the refusal of payment raises the possibility that personal resentment also played a part.
